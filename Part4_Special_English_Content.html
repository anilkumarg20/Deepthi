Chapter 1: Advanced Concord & Subject-Verb Agreement

Subject-Verb Agreement (Concord) is the most heavily tested grammar topic in competitive English teacher exams. Below is the complete catalog of advanced concord rules with precise exam examples.

1.1 Advanced Concord Rules

  1. When two subjects are joined by as well as, along with, together with, in addition to, with, like, unlike, accompanied by, besides, raw, rather than, the verb agrees with the **first subject**.
    Example: "The prime minister, accompanied by his cabinet members, has arrived."
  2. When two subjects are joined by either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, or, the verb agrees with the **nearest subject**.
    Example: "Neither the captain nor the players were happy with the decision."
  3. If the subject consists of each, every, everyone, someone, somebody, nobody, anyone, either, neither, it takes a **singular verb**.
    Example: "Neither of the two roads leads to the railway station."
  4. Many a, More than one are followed by a singular noun and take a **singular verb**.
    Example: "Many a soldier was killed in the battle."
  5. Nouns plural in form but singular in meaning (e.g., physics, mathematics, measles, news, innings, summons) take a **singular verb**.
    Example: "A summons was served on him yesterday."
  6. Nouns like scissors, trousers, spectacles, pliers, tongs, binoculars are treated as plural unless preceded by "a pair of".
    Example: "My spectacles have gone missing." vs. "A pair of spectacles has been found."
  7. Collective nouns (e.g., committee, jury, audience, crowd, class) take a singular verb when acting as a single unit, but a plural verb when members act individually.
    Example: "The committee is planning the annual event." vs. "The committee are arguing among themselves."
  8. If two singular nouns refer to the same person or thing (indicated by a single article before the first noun), the verb is **singular**.
    Example: "The secretary and treasurer has resigned." vs. "The secretary and the treasurer have resigned."

1.2 Conditional Clauses (If Clauses Matrix)

Conditional sentences consist of an 'If-clause' (condition) and a 'Main-clause' (result). The four structural types must be memorized:

Conditional Type If-Clause Verb Tense Main-Clause Verb Structure Example Sentence
Type 0 (Zero) Simple Present ($V_1$) Simple Present ($V_1$) If you heat ice, it melts. (Scientific truth)
Type 1 (Probable) Simple Present ($V_1$) will/shall/can/may + $V_1$ If it rains, we will cancel the match.
Type 2 (Imaginary) Simple Past ($V_2$) / were would/could/should + $V_1$ If I were a king, I would help the poor.
Type 3 (Unreal Past) Past Perfect (had + $V_3$) would/could + have + $V_3$ If she had studied, she would have passed.

1.3 Exceptional Question Tag Rules

  • Seminegative Statements: Statements containing words like hardly, scarcely, barely, rarely, seldom, few, little are negative. Their tag must be **positive**.
    Example: "He seldom visits his parents, does he?"
    Example: "There is little water in the glass, is there?"
  • Indefinite Subjects: When the subject of the sentence is everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody, the pronoun in the tag is **they**.
    Example: "Someone has stolen my pen, haven't they?"
    Example: "Nobody called me, did they?" (Nobody is negative, so the tag is positive).
  • Let's vs. Let us:
    Recommendations starting with "Let's" always take the tag **shall we** (e.g., "Let's go, shall we?").
    Requests starting with "Let us" (meaning allow us) take the tag **will you** (e.g., "Let us go, will you?").

Chapter 2: Non-Finite Verbs, Inversion & Sentence Synthesis

2.1 Non-Finite Verbs: Infinitives, Gerunds & Participles

Non-finite verbs do not change their form according to tense, number, or person of the subject. They are divided into three types:

  • Infinitives:
    • To-Infinitive: "To + $V_1$". Acts as a noun, adjective, or adverb. (e.g., "To err is human", "I want to study").
    • Bare Infinitive: Infinitive without "to". Used after modal auxiliary verbs (can, may, must) and verbs like **let, make, bid, see, hear, watch**.
      Example: "He made me cry" (not *to cry*). "Let him go."
  • Gerunds (Verbal Nouns): "$V_1 + \text{ing}$" acting as a noun.
    Example: "Smoking is injurious to health" (Subject). "I like reading" (Object).
    Commonly used after specific expressions: **look forward to, used to, prone to, object to, with a view to**.
    Example: "I am looking forward to meeting you."
  • Participles (Verbal Adjectives): Act as adjectives or help form compound tenses.
    • Present Participle ($V_{ing}$): Indicates ongoing action. (e.g., "A barking dog seldom bites").
    • Past Participle ($V_3$): Indicates completed action. (e.g., "A broken chair is useless").
    • Perfect Participle (Having + $V_3$): Indicates one action completed before another begins. (e.g., "Having completed his homework, he went to play").

2.2 Word Order & Grammatical Inversion

Inversion occurs when the auxiliary verb is placed before the subject. This is commonly tested in teacher exams using negative or restrictive adverbs at the beginning of sentences:

  • No sooner...than:
    Incorrect: "No sooner I had entered the room than the phone rang."
    Correct: "No sooner had I entered the room than the phone rang." (or "No sooner did I enter...").
  • Hardly/Scarcely/Barely...when:
    Incorrect: "Hardly she had finished her speech when the audience applauded."
    Correct: "Hardly had she finished her speech when the audience applauded."
  • Seldom/Rarely/Little:
    Incorrect: "Seldom we see such talent in a young artist."
    Correct: "Seldom do we see such talent in a young artist."
  • Under no circumstances / On no account:
    Incorrect: "Under no circumstances you should open this door."
    Correct: "Under no circumstances should you open this door."

2.3 Synthesis of Sentences

Combining two or more simple sentences into a single sentence (Simple, Compound, or Complex):

  • Combining into a Simple Sentence:
    • Using a Participle: "He saw the police. He ran away." $\rightarrow$ "Seeing the police, he ran away."
    • Using an Infinitive: "He wants to pass. He is working hard." $\rightarrow$ "He is working hard to pass."
    • Using a Nominative Absolute: "The sun set. The birds returned to their nests." $\rightarrow$ "The sun having set, the birds returned to their nests."
  • Combining into a Compound Sentence:
    Using Coordinating Conjunctions: "He is poor. He is honest." $\rightarrow$ "He is poor but honest." OR "He is not only poor but also honest."
  • Combining into a Complex Sentence:
    Using Subordinating Conjunctions: "He was sick. He did not come." $\rightarrow$ "As he was sick, he did not come." OR "He did not come because he was sick."

Chapter 3: Figures of Speech & Literary Elements

For secondary and senior English language teachers, identifying literary devices and forms is key. Below is the comprehensive database of figures of speech, foreign phrases, and literary genres.

3.1 Figures of Speech (Literary Devices)

  • Simile: A direct comparison between two distinct things using "like" or "as".
    Example: "He is as brave as a lion." "Her cheeks are like roses."
  • Metaphor: An indirect or implied comparison without using "like" or "as".
    Example: "He is a lion in the fight." "The camel is the ship of the desert."
  • Personification: Giving human qualities or characteristics to inanimate objects or abstract ideas.
    Example: "Death lays his icy hands on kings." "The wind whispered in the night."
  • Alliteration: The repetition of the same consonant sound at the beginning of adjacent or closely connected words.
    Example: "She sells seashells by the seashore."
  • Onomatopoeia: Words that imitate the natural sound associated with them.
    Example: "The buzzing bee flew past." "The hiss of the snake."
  • Hyperbole: An extreme exaggeration for emphasis or rhetorical effect.
    Example: "I am so hungry I could eat a horse." "She wept oceans of tears."
  • Irony: The expression of one's meaning by using language that normally signifies the opposite, typically for humorous or emphatic effect.
    Example: "The fire station burned down." "What a beautiful day!" (during a hurricane).
  • Oxymoron: A figure of speech in which apparently contradictory terms appear in conjunction.
    Example: "Bitter-sweet," "deafening silence," "open secret," "seriously funny."
  • Synecdoche: A figure of speech in which a part is made to represent the whole or vice versa.
    Example: "All hands on deck" (hands = sailors). "He bought a new set of wheels" (wheels = car).
  • Metonymy: Substituting the name of an attribute or feature for that of the thing meant.
    Example: "The pen is mightier than the sword" (pen = writing, sword = military force). "The White House announced..." (White House = administration).
  • Transferred Epithet: An adjective (epithet) grammatically qualifies a noun other than the person or thing it is actually describing.
    Example: "He passed a sleepless night" (The night wasn't sleepless, the person was).

3.3 Literary Forms (Genres of Literature)

  • Sonnet: A 14-line lyric poem written in iambic pentameter with a fixed rhyme scheme.
    • Shakespearean (English) Sonnet: Three quatrains and a concluding couplet (Rhyme scheme: abab cdcd efef gg).
    • Petrarchan (Italian) Sonnet: An octave (8 lines) followed by a sestet (6 lines) (Rhyme scheme: abbaabba followed by cdecde or cdcdcd).
  • Ode: A formal, often ceremonious lyric poem that addresses and celebrates a person, place, thing, or idea.
  • Elegy: A poem of serious reflection, typically a lament for the dead (e.g., Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard").
  • Ballad: A narrative poem that tells a story, traditionally sung or recited, written in short stanzas (quatrains).
  • Dramatic Monologue: A poem in which a single speaker addresses a silent listener at a critical moment (e.g., Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess").
  • Tragedy & Comedy:
    • Tragedy: A serious drama depicting the downfall of a noble protagonist due to a fatal flaw (hamartia) (e.g., Hamlet, Macbeth).
    • Comedy: A lighthearted drama intended to amuse, usually ending in a happy resolution (marriage).

Chapter 4: Advanced Special Content Practice Questions

Q1. Identify the figure of speech: "The pen is mightier than the sword."
A) Metaphor
B) Synecdoche
C) Metonymy
D) Oxymoron

Answer: C. "Pen" represents writing/journalism and "sword" represents military force. Substituting an associated attribute is Metonymy.

Q2. Complete the sentence using the correct verb: "Neither the teacher nor the students _____ present in the laboratory."
A) was
B) were
C) has been
D) is

Answer: B. Under the rule of proximity for "neither...nor", the verb agrees with the nearest subject "students" (plural), hence "were".

Q3. Choose the correct spelling for the blank: "The committee adjourned _____."
A) sine die
B) sine dy
C) sign die
D) sin die

Answer: A. "sine die" (Latin for "without a day") is the correct spelling.
