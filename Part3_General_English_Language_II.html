1. Vocabulary (Exhaustive Coverage)

This chapter compiles all 14 vocabulary topics specified in the official AP TET English Language II syllabus with definitions, classifications, rules, and examples.

1.2 Homophones & Homonyms

  • Homophones: Words with the same sound/pronunciation but different spellings and meanings.
    Examples:
    • Principal (Head of school / Main) vs. Principle (Fundamental rule).
    • Stationary (Not moving) vs. Stationery (Writing materials).
    • Altar (Sacred place in worship) vs. Alter (To change).
  • Homonyms: Words with the same spelling and sound/pronunciation but different meanings depending on context.
    Examples:
    • Bank: Financial institution / Edge of a river.
    • Bat: Flying mammal / Sports equipment.
    • Fair: Just/equitable / Exhibition / Light-skinned.

1.3 Idioms and Phrases

Expressions whose meanings cannot be derived from the literal definitions of the individual words.

  • At the eleventh hour: At the very last moment (e.g., "He submitted the exam notes at the eleventh hour").
  • Beat around the bush: Avoid addressing the main topic (e.g., "Get straight to the point instead of beating around the bush").
  • Bite the bullet: Face a difficult situation with courage (e.g., "He had to bite the bullet and tell the truth").

1.4 Phrasal Verbs

A verb combined with an adverb or preposition to create a new idiomatic meaning.

  • Break down: To stop functioning (e.g., "Our car broke down on the highway").
  • Call off: To cancel (e.g., "The teacher decided to call off the test").
  • Look after: To take care of (e.g., "She looks after her younger brother").
  • Put up with: To tolerate (e.g., "I cannot put up with this noise anymore").

1.6 Word Formation (Prefixes, Suffixes & Conversions)

  • Prefixation: Adding a morpheme to the beginning of a root word to change meaning (e.g., *happy* $\rightarrow$ unhappy, *polite* $\rightarrow$ impolite).
  • Suffixation: Adding a morpheme to the end of a root word (e.g., *develop* $\rightarrow$ development, *beauty* $\rightarrow$ beautiful).
  • Word Conversion:
    • Verb to Noun: **Decide** (Verb) $\rightarrow$ **Decision** (Noun); **Perform** $\rightarrow$ **Performance**.
    • Noun to Adjective: **Beauty** (Noun) $\rightarrow$ **Beautiful** (Adjective); **Danger** $\rightarrow$ **Dangerous**.
    • Verb to Adjective: **Create** (Verb) $\rightarrow$ **Creative** (Adjective).
  • Compound Words: Combining two or more words to form a new word (e.g., sun + flower = sunflower, black + board = blackboard).
  • Unscrambling Letters: Re-arranging jumbled letters to form valid dictionary words (e.g., *t-e-a-c-h-r-e* $\rightarrow$ **teacher**).

1.7 Singular - Plural Words

Identifying standard and irregular plural formations:

  • Standard: Boy $\rightarrow$ Boys, Box $\rightarrow$ Boxes, Baby $\rightarrow$ Babies.
  • Irregular: Man $\rightarrow$ Men, Child $\rightarrow$ Children, Tooth $\rightarrow$ Teeth, Mouse $\rightarrow$ Mice.
  • Latin/Greek Roots: Datum $\rightarrow$ **Data**, Crisis $\rightarrow$ **Crises**, Phenomenon $\rightarrow$ **Phenomena**, Syllabus $\rightarrow$ **Syllabi**.

1.8 One-Word Substitutes

  • Infallible: One who never makes mistakes.
  • Atheist: A person who does not believe in the existence of God.
  • Honorary: A post for which no salary is paid.
  • Omniscient: One who knows everything.

1.9 Collocations

Words that naturally go together in English conversation and writing.

  • Do: Do homework, do a favor, do business (not *make homework*).
  • Make: Make a mistake, make a bed, make progress (not *do a mistake*).
  • Fast vs. Quick: **Fast food**, **fast train** (not *quick food*) vs. **quick shower**, **quick glance** (not *fast glance*).

1.10 Ordinal Numbers

Numbers indicating position or rank rather than quantity (Cardinal: 1, 2, 3 vs. Ordinal: **First**, **Second**, **Third**, **Fourth**).

1.11 Anagrams

Words formed by rearranging the letters of another word (e.g., **listen** $\rightarrow$ **silent**, **elbow** $\rightarrow$ **below**, **state** $\rightarrow$ **taste**).

1.12 Figures of Speech

  • Simile: Direct comparison using 'like' or 'as' (e.g., "as brave as a lion").
  • Metaphor: Indirect/implied comparison (e.g., "He is a lion in the fight").
  • Personification: Giving human qualities to objects (e.g., "The wind whispered in the trees").

2. Grammar (18 Crucial Sub-Topics)

Below is the complete, advanced breakdown of all 18 grammatical sub-topics specified in the official syllabus syllabus sheet:

2.1 Parts of Speech (All Types, Functions & Usage)

The eight parts of speech and their primary functions:

  • Nouns: Names of people, places, things, or abstract ideas.
    Types: Proper (Ravi), Common (city), Collective (flock, band), Material (iron), Abstract (kindness).
  • Pronouns: Words replacing nouns.
    Types: Personal (I, he), Reflexive (myself), Demonstrative (this, those), Relative (who, which).
  • Verbs: Express actions, states, or occurrences.
    Types: Finite (changes with tense) vs. Non-finite (does not change: gerunds, infinitives).
  • Adjectives: Describe nouns (e.g., "the intelligent boy").
  • Adverbs: Modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs (e.g., "walked slowly").
  • Prepositions: Establish space, time, or direction relations (e.g., on, under, at, in).
  • Conjunctions: Connect clauses or words.
    Types: Coordinating (and, but), Subordinating (although, since), Correlative (either...or).
  • Interjections: Express sudden emotions (e.g., Alas!, Hurrah!).

2.2 Articles

  • Indefinite Articles (A/An): Used with singular countable nouns mentioned for the first time.
    Rules: Use **an** before vowel sounds (e.g., an hour, an honest man, an MLA) and **a** before consonant sounds (e.g., a university, a European, a one-eyed man).
  • Definite Article (The): Used for specific nouns, geographical features, unique objects, superlatives, and musical instruments (e.g., the Sun, the Ganges, the tallest boy).
  • Omission of Articles: Do not use articles before names of languages, meals, sports, cities, and abstract nouns used in a general sense (e.g., "Gold is a precious metal," "I play tennis").

2.3 Tenses (Forms, Structures & Usages)

Tense Category Positive Structure Negative Structure Usage / Time Markers
Simple Present $S + V_1(s/es) + O$ $S + \text{do/does not} + V_1 + O$ Habitual actions, universal truths (e.g., "always", "every day").
Present Continuous $S + \text{is/am/are} + V_{ing} + O$ $S + \text{is/am/are not} + V_{ing} + O$ Action in progress right now (e.g., "now", "at present").
Present Perfect $S + \text{has/have} + V_3 + O$ $S + \text{has/have not} + V_3 + O$ Action just completed (e.g., "just now", "already", "yet").
Simple Past $S + V_2 + O$ $S + \text{did not} + V_1 + O$ Completed past actions (e.g., "yesterday", "ago", "last week").
Past Continuous $S + \text{was/were} + V_{ing} + O$ $S + \text{was/were not} + V_{ing} + O$ Action in progress in the past (e.g., "while", "when").
Past Perfect $S + \text{had} + V_3 + O$ $S + \text{had not} + V_3 + O$ The earlier of two past actions (e.g., "before", "after").
Simple Future $S + \text{will/shall} + V_1 + O$ $S + \text{will/shall not} + V_1 + O$ Future plans or predictions (e.g., "tomorrow", "next year").

2.4 Voice (Active to Passive Voice Conversion)

Passive voice places the focus on the receiver of the action rather than the agent:

  • Active: $S + V + O \rightarrow$ Passive: $O + \text{be-verb} + V_3 + \text{by} + S$.
  • Imperative Sentences:
    "Shut the window" $\rightarrow$ "Let the window be shut."
    "Don't write answers" $\rightarrow$ "Let answers not be written."
  • Double Objects:
    "He gave me a pen" $\rightarrow$ "I was given a pen by him" OR "A pen was given to me by him."

2.5 Reported Speech (Direct & Indirect Speech)

Modifications of tenses, pronouns, and time adverbs when reporting words directly or indirectly:

  • Assertive: "He said, 'I am busy'" $\rightarrow$ "He said that he was busy."
  • Interrogative (Yes/No): "She said, 'Are you coming?'" $\rightarrow$ "She asked if I was coming."
  • Interrogative (Wh-): "He said, 'Where is the school?'" $\rightarrow$ "He asked where the school was."
  • Imperative (Command/Request): "The doctor said, 'Take this medicine'" $\rightarrow$ "The doctor advised me to take that medicine."
  • Exclamatory: "She said, 'Alas! I lost my keys'" $\rightarrow$ "She exclaimed with sorrow that she had lost her keys."
  • Optative Sentences (Wishes/Prayers):
    Direct: "The priest said, 'May God bless you!'"
    Indirect: "The priest **prayed that God might bless me**."
    Direct: "He said to me, 'Good morning!'"
    Indirect: "He **wished me a good morning**."

2.6 Types of Sentences (Form & Purpose)

  • Assertive / Declarative: Makes a statement (e.g., "The sun rises in the east").
  • Interrogative: Asks a question (e.g., "Do you study English?").
  • Imperative: Gives a command or request (e.g., "Please sit down").
  • Exclamatory: Expresses sudden strong emotion (e.g., "What a beautiful building!").

2.7 Degrees of Comparison

  • Type 1:
    Positive: No other animal is as large as the elephant.
    Comparative: The elephant is larger than any other animal.
    Superlative: The elephant is the largest animal.
  • Type 2:
    Positive: Very few cities are as crowded as Tokyo.
    Comparative: Tokyo is more crowded than most other cities.
    Superlative: Tokyo is one of the most crowded cities.

2.8 Linkers (Cohesion & Coherence)

Connective words indicating relationship between ideas:

  • Addition: furthermore, in addition to, besides.
  • Contrast: however, although, on the contrary, yet.
  • Cause & Effect: therefore, consequently, as a result, since.

2.9 Language Functions (Communicative Purpose)

Recognizing the social purpose of spoken expressions in context:

  • "Could you please lend me your pen?" $\rightarrow$ Making a request.
  • "I am extremely sorry for the delay." $\rightarrow$ Apologizing.
  • "Would you like to join us for dinner?" $\rightarrow$ Inviting.
  • "Congratulations on your spectacular achievement!" $\rightarrow$ Congratulating.

2.10 Agreement of Subject with Verb (Concord)

  • Singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs.
  • "The captain, along with the team members, is present" (Verb agrees with the first subject).
  • "Neither the teacher nor the students were satisfied" (Verb agrees with the nearest subject).

2.11 Helping Verbs / Modal Auxiliaries

Primary auxiliary verbs (be, do, have) and modal verbs (can, could, may, might, must, should, would):

  • Can: Ability (e.g., "I can swim").
  • May: Permission / Possibility (e.g., "May I come in?").
  • Must: Obligation / Necessity (e.g., "You must wear a helmet").
  • Contracted Negatives: Do not $\rightarrow$ **don't**; Cannot $\rightarrow$ **can't**; Will not $\rightarrow$ **won't**; Shall not $\rightarrow$ **shanj't**.

2.12 Framing Wh-Questions

Syntactic rule: $\text{Wh-word} + \text{helping verb} + \text{subject} + \text{main verb} + \text{object?}$
Example: "He lives in Hyderabad" $\rightarrow$ "**Where does he live?**" (not *Where he lives?*).
Example: "She has finished her work" $\rightarrow$ "**What has she finished?**".

2.13 Order of Adjectives

Standard arrangement sequence when multiple adjectives modify a noun:
$\text{Opinion} \rightarrow \text{Size} \rightarrow \text{Age} \rightarrow \text{Shape} \rightarrow \text{Color} \rightarrow \text{Origin} \rightarrow \text{Material} \rightarrow \text{Purpose}$.
Example: "A **beautiful** (opinion), **large** (size), **old** (age), **wooden** (material) chest."

2.14 Transitive and Intransitive Verbs

  • Transitive: Actions that transfer to a direct object (e.g., "Ravi **bought** a car").
  • Intransitive: Actions that do not pass to an object (e.g., "The baby **slept** soundly").

2.15 Question Tags

  • Positive sentences take negative tags; negative sentences take positive tags.
  • "You are reading, **aren't you?**"
  • "They don't know the answer, **do they?**"
  • "I am a teacher, **aren't I?**" (Exceptional tag).
  • "Let's play, **shall we?**"

2.16 Common Errors

  • Incorrect: "He is my cousin brother." $\rightarrow$ Correct: "He is my **cousin**."
  • Incorrect: "I prefer tea than coffee." $\rightarrow$ Correct: "I prefer tea **to** coffee."
  • Incorrect: "She is suffering with fever." $\rightarrow$ Correct: "She is suffering **from** fever."

2.17 Transformation & Synthesis of Sentences

  • Simple Sentence: One independent clause. (e.g., "Due to illness, he was absent").
  • Compound Sentence: Two independent clauses connected by a coordinating conjunction. (e.g., "He was ill, **and so** he was absent").
  • Complex Sentence: One independent clause and one or more subordinate clauses. (e.g., "He was absent **because** he was ill").

2.18 Clauses

  • If Clauses:
    • Type I: If she runs fast, she will win (Probable).
    • Type II: If she ran fast, she would win (Imaginary).
    • Type III: If she had run fast, she would have won (Unreal past).
  • Subordinate Clauses:
    • Noun Clause: "I believe **that he is honest**."
    • Adverbial Clause: "We went inside **when it started raining**."
    • Relative/Adjective Clause: "The girl **who sang yesterday** is my sister."
  • Finite vs. Non-Finite Clauses: Finite clauses contain a verb marked for tense/person (e.g., "He went"); Non-finite clauses contain participles or infinitives (e.g., "Entering the room").
  • Subject and Predicate: The subject is the naming part (e.g., "The dog"); the predicate is the action/description part (e.g., "barked at the mailman").

3. Conventions of Writing

Conventions of writing include the rules of orthography, punctuation, and capitalization that make text readable and coherent:

  • Capital Letters: Used at the beginning of sentences, for proper nouns (Ravi, Hyderabad), the pronoun 'I', titles, and abbreviations (MLA, USA).
  • Full Stop (.): Indicates the end of a declarative or imperative sentence. Also used in abbreviations (e.g., Prof., Dr.).
  • Comma (,): Separates items in a list, clauses in a compound/complex sentence, and parenthetical elements (e.g., "Yes, I will go," "If it rains, we will stop").
  • Question Mark (?): Placed at the end of direct questions (e.g., "Where are you going?"). Do not use for indirect questions (e.g., "He asked where I was going.").
  • Exclamation Mark (!): Expresses strong emotion, surprise, or commands (e.g., "What a tragedy!", "Stop!").
  • Inverted Commas (" "): Encloses direct speech and quotes (e.g., He said, "I am ready.").
  • Apostrophe ('): Indicates possession (e.g., John's book, players' bags) and contractions (e.g., don't, it's).

4. Discourses

Discourses refer to written or spoken communication formats. The AP TET syllabus requires knowledge of the structure and features of these 8 key formats:

4.1 Letter Writing

  • Formal Letters: Sent to authorities, business partners, or editors.
    Format: Sender's Address $\rightarrow$ Date $\rightarrow$ Receiver's Designation/Address $\rightarrow$ Subject $\rightarrow$ Salutation (Dear Sir/Madam) $\rightarrow$ Body of Letter $\rightarrow$ Subscription (Yours faithfully/sincerely) $\rightarrow$ Signature.
  • Informal Letters: Sent to family or friends.
    Format: Sender's Address $\rightarrow$ Date $\rightarrow$ Salutation (My dear Ravi) $\rightarrow$ Body $\rightarrow$ Subscription (Yours lovingly / With love) $\rightarrow$ Signature.

4.2 Diary Entry

A personal record of thoughts, feelings, and events written in a conversational, informal tone:

  • Structure: Date, Day, and Time on top $\rightarrow$ Salutation (Dear Diary - optional) $\rightarrow$ First-person narrative ("I") $\rightarrow$ Honest expression of feelings $\rightarrow$ Signature (optional).

4.3 E-mail Communication

Electronic mail format for quick communication:

  • Fields: **To** (Recipient's address), **Cc** (Carbon copy for visibility), **Bcc** (Blind carbon copy for private visibility), **Subject** (Short summary), **Body** (Greeting, main text, call to action), **Attachments** (if any).

4.4 Paragraph Writing

A cohesive unit of text focusing on a single topic or theme:

  • Structure: **Topic Sentence** (introduces the main idea) $\rightarrow$ **Supporting Sentences** (provide evidence, details, examples) $\rightarrow$ **Concluding Sentence** (summarizes the main point or transitions to the next paragraph).

4.5 Dialogue Writing

Formatting written conversations between two or more people:

  • Rules: Write the speaker's name followed by a colon. Enclose actions in brackets. Punctuate natural pauses.
    Example:
    **Ravi:** (smiling) Hello, how have you been?
    **Lata:** I've been great! How about you?

4.6 Notice & Invitation

  • Notice: Formally alerts a group about an upcoming event.
    Format: Place the word "NOTICE" at the top center $\rightarrow$ Name of issuing organization $\rightarrow$ Date $\rightarrow$ Heading $\rightarrow$ Body (covering Who, What, When, Where, Why) $\rightarrow$ Name and designation of the issuer. Enclosed in a **rectangular box**.
  • Invitation: Formally invites people to events.
    Format: Host's name $\rightarrow$ Invitation formula (e.g., "cordially invites you to...") $\rightarrow$ Event name $\rightarrow$ Date, Time, and Venue $\rightarrow$ RSVP (Reply details).

4.7 Biographical Sketch

A short profile of a person's life, career, and achievements written by someone else:

  • Structure: Full name as title $\rightarrow$ Birth date/place and family background $\rightarrow$ Education and career path $\rightarrow$ Major accomplishments/contributions $\rightarrow$ Death details (if applicable) $\rightarrow$ Impact on society.

4.8 Speech Writing

A written address to be spoken before an audience:

  • Structure: **Salutation & Hook** (greeting the chief guest and audience) $\rightarrow$ **Introduction of Topic** $\rightarrow$ **Body Paragraphs** (points backed by statistics/logic) $\rightarrow$ **Conclusion** (a final call to action or summary) $\rightarrow$ **Thank you**.

5. Dictionary Skill

5.1 Alphabetical Order

Arranging word lists based on standard dictionary sequence. In exams, you are tested on sorting words with identical starting letters:

  • Compare letter by letter: **Apple** $\rightarrow$ **Apricot** $\rightarrow$ **Avocado** (based on second/third letters).
  • Example sort: *Teacher, Tenant, Tear, Team* $\rightarrow$ **Team, Tear, Teacher, Tenant**.

5.2 Dictionary Entry Analysis

Understanding the standard components of a dictionary layout:

  • Headword: The word being looked up (in bold).
  • Pronunciation: Shown in IPA phonetic symbols (e.g., /tʃeə(r)/).
  • Part of Speech: Grammatical category (e.g., *n.* for noun, *v.* for verb).
  • Definition: Explanations of meanings, often numbered for multiple senses.
  • Guide Words: Words printed at the top of a page showing the first and last entries on that page.

6. Reading Comprehension

  • Seen Passages: Passages directly taken from the Grade 1 to 10 English textbooks. Questions test direct facts, vocabulary, and theme recall.
  • Unseen Passages: Informational, narrative, or factual prose from outside the textbooks. Questions test inference, skimming, scanning, and vocabulary.
  • Poem Comprehension: Reading short stanzas (textual or unseen) to identify poetic devices, rhyming words, and theme interpretation.

7. Phonetics (All 9 official sub-topics)

Phonetics is the study of speech sounds. Below are the nine areas specified in the TET syllabus:

7.1 Vowel & Consonant Sounds

The English language has 44 sounds, split into Vowels and Consonants:

  • Vowels (20 sounds): Sounds produced without obstruction in the vocal tract.
    • Monophthongs (12): Pure vowels (e.g., /i:/ in *see*, /ɪ/ in *sit*, /æ/ in *cat*).
    • Diphthongs (8): Gliding vowels (e.g., /aɪ/ in *my*, /eɪ/ in *day*).
  • Consonants (24 sounds): Sounds produced by obstructing the airflow.

7.2 Voiced and Voiceless Sounds

  • Voiceless Sounds: Produced without vocal cord vibration (9 consonant sounds):
    /p/ (pen), /t/ (tea), /k/ (key), /f/ (fan), /θ/ (thin), /s/ (sip), /ʃ/ (ship), /h/ (hat), /tʃ/ (cheap).
  • Voiced Sounds: Produced with vocal cord vibration (all 20 vowels + remaining 15 consonants):
    /b/ (boy), /d/ (dog), /g/ (go), /v/ (van), /ð/ (this), /z/ (zip), /ʒ/ (measure), /dʒ/ (jeep), /m/ (man), /n/ (net), /ŋ/ (sing), /l/ (light), /r/ (run), /w/ (wet), /j/ (yes).

7.3 Place & Manner of Articulation

How and where speech sounds are produced by organs of speech:

  • Bilabial: Produced using both lips (e.g., /p/, /b/, /m/, /w/).
  • Labiodental: Lower lip touching upper teeth (e.g., /f/, /v/).
  • Dental: Tongue touching teeth (e.g., /θ/, /ð/).
  • Alveolar: Tongue touching tooth ridge (e.g., /t/, /d/, /s/, /z/, /n/, /l/).
  • Velar: Back of tongue touching soft palate (e.g., /k/, /g/, /ŋ/).
  • Glottal: Produced in the throat/vocal cords (e.g., /h/).

7.4 Phonemes

A phoneme is the smallest unit of sound that distinguishes one word from another (e.g., **cat** has 3 phonemes: /k/ - /æ/ - /t/; **chat** has 3 phonemes: /tʃ/ - /æ/ - /t/).

7.5 Morphemes

A morpheme is the smallest grammatical unit of meaning in a language:

  • Free Morphemes: Can stand alone as independent words (e.g., *play*, *book*, *happy*).
  • Bound Morphemes: Cannot stand alone; must attach to other morphemes (prefixes and suffixes like *-ful*, *un-*, *-ed*).
    Example: "unhappiness" contains 3 morphemes: **un-** (bound) + **happy** (free) + **-ness** (bound).

7.6 Syllable Identification & Classification

Syllables are units of speech organized around a vowel sound:

  • Monosyllabic (1 Syllable): Pen, cat, dog, go.
  • Disyllabic (2 Syllables): Teach-er, wa-ter, win-dow.
  • Trisyllabic (3 Syllables): Beau-ti-ful, to-mor-row.
  • Polysyllabic (4+ Syllables): Con-gra-tu-late, ex-am-i-na-tion.

7.7 Stress & Intonation

  • Stress: Emphasizing a particular syllable in a word. Two-syllable nouns stress the first syllable (**ob**ject); two-syllable verbs stress the second (ob**ject**).
  • Intonation: The rise and fall of pitch in spoken sentences:
    • Falling Intonation: Used in statements, commands, and Wh-questions (e.g., "What is your name? $\searrow$").
    • Rising Intonation: Used in Yes/No questions and polite requests (e.g., "Are you coming? $\nearrow$").

7.8 Silent Letters

Identifying unpronounced letters in standard written words:

  • Silent 'b': Doubt, climb, comb, debt.
  • Silent 'k': Know, knee, knife, knight.
  • Silent 'p': Psychology, pneumonia, receipt.
  • Silent 'l': Walk, talk, should, half.
  • Silent 'w': Write, wrong, answer.

7.9 Odd Sounds

Identifying the word whose underlined letters make a different sound compared to the others in the group:

Example 1: Identify the odd vowel sound:
A) sun
B) fun
C) put
D) run

Answer: C (put /ʊ/ has a different vowel sound compared to sun, fun, run which have /ʌ/).

Example 2: Identify the odd consonant sound:
A) character
B) chemistry
C) chorus
D) chair

Answer: D (chair /tʃ/ has a different consonant sound compared to character, chemistry, chorus which have /k/).
